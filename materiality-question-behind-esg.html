Materiality: The Question Behind ESG What's the Difference?

Three terms. Three different histories. One increasingly connected business agenda.

In this guide

Materiality is one of the most important concepts in sustainability management. It helps an organisation decide which impacts, risks and opportunities deserve attention, resources and accountability. Yet materiality is often treated as a reporting exercise: identify a long list of topics, engage stakeholders, produce a matrix and include the result in the sustainability report. That is where the real work can get lost.

01 — Why materiality matters

Every organisation faces more sustainability issues than it can address with equal intensity. Climate change, water, biodiversity, labour conditions, human rights, community relations, business ethics and governance may all be relevant. But their significance differs according to the organisation's activities, locations, value chain and business model.

Materiality provides a disciplined way to distinguish between issues that deserve management attention and those that do not have the same level of significance.

This is not simply a question of which topics are popular or frequently mentioned by stakeholders. It is a question of understanding the organisation's actual and potential impacts on people and the environment, as well as the sustainability-related risks and opportunities that may affect its prospects.

Materiality should help management decide what matters, why it matters, and what the organisation needs to do about it.

02 — Two perspectives on materiality

The term materiality is used in different ways across sustainability reporting standards. Two perspectives are particularly important.

01

Impact materiality

What significant impacts does the organisation have, or could it have, on people, the environment and the economy? This includes actual and potential positive and negative impacts, including human rights impacts.

02

Financial materiality

Which sustainability-related risks and opportunities could reasonably be expected to affect the organisation's financial prospects, performance, position, cash flows or access to finance?

03

Double materiality

A combined approach considering both the organisation's impacts on the world and the financial effects that sustainability matters may have on the organisation.

The Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) Standards focus on an organisation's most significant impacts on the economy, environment and people, including human rights.

The IFRS Sustainability Disclosure Standards, particularly IFRS S1, focus on sustainability-related risks and opportunities that could reasonably be expected to affect an entity's prospects.

The European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS) apply the concept of double materiality. A sustainability matter can be material from an impact perspective, a financial perspective, or both.

These approaches serve related but distinct purposes. They should not be treated as interchangeable definitions.

03 — How should a company conduct a materiality assessment?

A credible assessment begins with understanding the organisation, not with sending out a stakeholder questionnaire.

Step 1 — Understand the business

Start with the organisation's context

Understand the business model, strategy, operations, geographic footprint, products, services and value chain. Consider the sectors and locations in which the organisation operates.

Step 2 — Identify relevant matters

Look beyond the obvious topics

Identify actual and potential impacts, risks and opportunities. Use operational information, existing assessments, legal requirements, scientific evidence and sector knowledge.

Step 3 — Engage stakeholders

Listen to people who understand the consequences

Engage affected stakeholders and other relevant groups. Their perspectives can reveal impacts that may not be visible through internal management systems or financial analysis alone.

Step 4 — Assess significance

Apply appropriate criteria

For negative impacts, consider severity, including scale, scope and irremediable character. For potential impacts, consider likelihood. For financial materiality, assess the potential magnitude and likelihood of financial effects.

Step 5 — Validate and act

Connect the findings to management decisions

Validate the results, document the rationale and assign responsibility. Translate material matters into strategy, risk management, targets, operational plans and governance oversight.

The methodology should be proportionate to the organisation and consistent with the reporting standards it applies. There is no substitute for understanding the actual context in which impacts and risks arise.

04 — Where companies often get it wrong

The difficulty is rarely a lack of sustainability topics. It is the way organisations interpret and use the results.

Confusing stakeholder interest with materiality

An issue receiving considerable attention is not automatically the most significant issue. Stakeholder engagement is an important input, but the organisation must still assess the nature and significance of the impacts, risks and opportunities.

Starting with a standard list

Using a generic ESG checklist can be useful as an initial reference. It cannot replace an assessment of the company's own operations, value chain and circumstances.

Letting the reporting team own the entire process

Sustainability teams may coordinate the assessment, but operations, finance, risk, human resources, procurement, legal and business leadership all have relevant responsibilities.

Treating the materiality matrix as the final result

A matrix can communicate priorities. It cannot demonstrate that those priorities have been integrated into business decisions.

A materiality matrix that does not influence strategy, investment, risk management or accountability is unlikely to deliver its full value.

05 — A mining industry example: BHP and the reality of materiality

Mining provides a useful illustration of why materiality must be considered in the context of a company's actual operations.

For a large mining group such as BHP, sustainability matters can extend well beyond the environmental footprint of an individual mine. They may include climate change, water management, biodiversity, tailings storage facilities, worker health and safety, Indigenous peoples, community relationships and conditions across the value chain.

These issues do not all become material for the same reason.

Water: an impact and an operational concern

Water use and water quality can have direct consequences for local communities and ecosystems. In water-stressed locations, they can also affect operational continuity, relationships with regulators and communities, and the reliability of production.

The impact perspective asks what the operation means for people and the environment. The financial perspective asks how water-related conditions could affect the company's prospects. Both perspectives matter, but they answer different questions.

Tailings: beyond technical compliance

Tailings storage facilities illustrate the connection between engineering, environmental and social consequences, risk management and governance. A failure can have severe and lasting consequences for people and the environment, as well as substantial financial and reputational implications.

The relevant management questions therefore extend beyond whether a facility meets technical requirements. They include oversight, risk controls, monitoring, emergency preparedness, accountability and the quality of information reaching the board.

Indigenous peoples and communities: relationships matter

Mining projects can affect land, livelihoods, cultural heritage, access to resources and community development. These impacts may have long time horizons and cannot always be understood through conventional financial indicators.

Engagement should therefore help the company understand the perspectives and rights of affected people, identify potential impacts and inform decisions before problems become difficult to resolve.

BHP's public sustainability disclosures provide material for examining how a major mining company identifies and discusses these types of matters. They are useful evidence of the issues the company reports on, but disclosure alone is not independent proof that every impact is effectively managed.

A comparison with Rio Tinto

Rio Tinto offers another useful point of comparison. Water stewardship, tailings management, biodiversity and relationships with communities and Indigenous peoples are also relevant to understanding the sustainability context of large-scale mining.

The value of comparing the two companies is not simply to count how many topics appear in their reports. It is to examine how each organisation explains the connection between its impacts, risks, business strategy, management systems and accountability.

For boards and investors, the more important question is whether material matters are reflected in decisions, controls, capital allocation and operational performance.

06 — What should boards and management challenge?

Materiality is not just a technical exercise for sustainability specialists. It has direct implications for corporate governance.

Boards should be able to understand why certain matters have been identified as material, how the assessment was conducted, whose perspectives were considered and what the organisation intends to do with the findings.

Management should be able to explain how material matters connect to business plans, risk management, investment decisions, performance indicators and responsibilities across the organisation.

A useful test is to compare the materiality assessment with the decisions the company actually makes. If an issue is described as highly significant but does not influence budgets, operational controls or management accountability, the organisation should be prepared to explain the gap.

The Meridian Perspective

Materiality is a management decision, not just a reporting exercise.

The quality of a materiality assessment should not be judged by the number of topics identified or the sophistication of its matrix.

It should be judged by whether the organisation has developed a credible understanding of its impacts, risks and opportunities, and whether that understanding changes the way the business is managed.

This is where the real challenge begins. A company may identify the right issues and still fail to address them effectively. The gap between identifying a priority and acting on it is a governance and management issue.

The question for leadership is not simply:

“Have we completed our materiality assessment?”

It is: “What decisions have changed because of what we learned?”

07 — Five questions every board should ask

01

Why are these matters considered material?

Can management explain the evidence, criteria and reasoning behind the priorities?

02

Whose perspectives have been considered?

Have affected stakeholders, including people who may have limited influence, been heard?

03

How do material matters affect our strategy and risk profile?

Are the findings reflected in business planning, risk management and investment decisions?

04

Who is accountable for managing them?

Are responsibilities, resources, targets and oversight clearly assigned?

05

What has changed as a result?

Can management demonstrate that the assessment has influenced actual decisions and actions?
