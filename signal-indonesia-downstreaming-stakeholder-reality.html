The difficult part of Indonesia's energy transition may not be the technology.

Indonesia wants more than a green economy. It wants an industrial economy. That distinction matters.

Meridian ESG · September 2026

Indonesia's energy transition is often discussed through the language of technology: batteries, electric vehicles, critical minerals, renewable energy and decarbonisation.

But there is another transition happening at the same time.

Indonesia is trying to move further up the value chain — processing minerals domestically, building industrial capacity, attracting investment and creating jobs rather than simply exporting raw materials.

Nickel sits directly in the middle of this story.

The country's downstreaming agenda has increasingly connected mining, processing, batteries and industrial development. Government policy is also placing greater emphasis on sustainability and the longer-term economic future of regions that host these activities.

In other words, the question is no longer simply whether Indonesia can produce the minerals needed for the energy transition.

The more interesting question is whether the economic, environmental and social systems around those projects can move in the same direction.

Four realities are meeting in the same project.

This is where the Indonesian conversation becomes more complicated.

01 · DEVELOPMENT

Industrial ambition

Downstreaming is not simply a mining policy. It is part of a broader ambition to build domestic industry, capture more value and create economic opportunities around Indonesia's natural resources.

02 · BUSINESS

Commercial reality

Projects still have to work economically. Investment, production, infrastructure, energy costs, commodity markets and supply-chain requirements do not disappear because a project has an ESG strategy.

03 · SUSTAINABILITY

Environmental reality

Emissions, biodiversity, water, reclamation, climate exposure and transition requirements increasingly affect how projects are viewed by regulators, investors and markets.

04 · STAKEHOLDERS

Social reality

Communities, local livelihoods, government, workers, civil society and other stakeholders experience a project very differently from the way it may appear in a board presentation.

These are often treated as separate workstreams.

In reality, they can become deeply connected.

A project can be economically important, technically compliant and strategically attractive — and still encounter a stakeholder problem that becomes a business problem.

Indonesia is already showing why this matters.

In June 2026, Indonesia's Ministry of Energy and Mineral Resources said that the government's review of 2026 nickel production plans was not simply a question of increasing or reducing quotas. The review involved industrial demand, downstream supply, market conditions, commodity prices and the country's remaining mineral reserves.

That is a useful reminder that the mining story is no longer only about extraction. It is about the resilience of the wider industrial system.

The same broader thinking is visible in Indonesia's sustainable-finance architecture. OJK's Sustainable Finance Taxonomy has evolved beyond a simple list of "green" activities, while the 2026 Sustainable Capital Market Roadmap is intended to strengthen sustainable financing and investment within Indonesia's capital market.

Meanwhile, ESDM has explicitly linked downstreaming with the longer-term economic future of host regions, including the need to think about what happens after a mining project eventually reaches the end of its life.

That last point is particularly interesting.

The Meridian Question

Can a project be economically successful, technically compliant and still struggle to maintain social legitimacy?

The answer is not as straightforward as an ESG checklist suggests.

Sustainability discussions often divide a project into categories: environmental, social and governance.

Useful as that may be for reporting, the reality on the ground does not always respect those boundaries.

A concern that begins as a community issue can become a permitting issue.

A permitting issue can become a production issue.

A production issue can become an investor issue.

And an investor issue can eventually become a strategic issue for the company.

The chain is not inevitable. But it is precisely why stakeholder intelligence matters.

Meridian Interpretation

The Indonesian sustainability conversation is sometimes framed as a choice between development and environmental responsibility.

That framing is too simple.

Indonesia is trying to achieve several things simultaneously: develop domestic industry, attract investment, create employment, maintain resource competitiveness, manage environmental impacts and retain the confidence of communities and other stakeholders.

Those objectives can support one another.

They can also pull in different directions.

The real challenge is not choosing between development and sustainability. It is understanding where they collide.

That is where stakeholder engagement becomes much more than a social programme or consultation exercise.

It becomes part of how business risk is understood.

In Indonesia, stakeholder reality can become business reality.

What should companies be watching?

Governance Who inside the organisation actually sees stakeholder signals before they become formal risks?
Strategy Does the sustainability strategy influence project decisions, or is it mainly designed around reporting requirements?
Local reality What does economic development mean to the communities living around the project — and is that understanding changing over time?
Transition What happens to the region when the economics of the resource, technology or project eventually change?

The hardest sustainability problems rarely arrive labelled "ESG."

They arrive as a community concern, a regulatory change, an investor question, a project delay, a reputational issue or a decision that suddenly has no easy answer.

That is why understanding the connection between the issues can matter more than understanding each issue separately.

For Indonesia's next phase of industrial development, that connection may become increasingly important.
