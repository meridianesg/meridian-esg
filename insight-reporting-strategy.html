A Good Sustainability Report Can Still Hide a Bad Sustainability Strategy

Sometimes the report looks better than the reality behind it. That is where the interesting questions begin.

Meridian ESG · Perspective
An observation
The report may tell you what happened. The harder question is what changed.

There is a moment that happens in many organisations.

The sustainability report is finally finished.

The numbers are there. The targets are there. The photographs are there. The language has been carefully reviewed. The charts look good.

Everyone can breathe again.

And then comes the question that is sometimes harder to answer:

The question that matters

Did anything actually change?

Not in the report.

In the business.

Did an investment decision change? Did a supplier conversation change? Did someone in operations do something differently? Did management make a difficult trade-off because of a sustainability issue?

Sometimes the answer is clearly yes.

Sometimes it is much less clear.

The uncomfortable gap between reporting and reality

Sustainability reporting has come a long way. That is a good thing.

Companies have become much better at measuring, disclosing and explaining what they are doing. Expectations from investors, regulators, customers, employees and other stakeholders have also changed.

But there is a trap here.

An organisation can become very good at describing sustainability without becoming equally good at managing it.

The two are connected, but they are not the same.

“The report can be finished in a few months. Changing the business can take years.”

Where things usually become interesting

Look beyond the report and into the decisions that keep the organisation running.

The target is impressive. But who owns it?

It is relatively easy to announce a target. It is harder to make sure that somebody in the business is actually responsible for delivering it — with the authority, resources and support to do so.

Sustainability has a seat at the table. But does it have a voice?

Some organisations have created committees, appointed sustainability leaders and introduced new reporting structures. All useful. But the real test comes when sustainability meets cost, growth, production or commercial pressure.

There is plenty of activity. But is the difficult issue being addressed?

Organisations can accumulate programmes, partnerships, campaigns and initiatives. The list can become impressive. Yet the most important issue may still be sitting quietly in the room.

This is where strategy matters

A sustainability strategy should not simply organise everything the company is already doing.

It should help the organisation decide what matters, what needs to change and where difficult choices may have to be made.

That can be uncomfortable.

Because once sustainability starts influencing real decisions, there are usually trade-offs.

A supplier may no longer be the easiest choice. A project may need to be redesigned. A growth opportunity may carry a risk that cannot simply be put into a footnote. A target may require money that was originally intended for something else.

That is when sustainability stops being a communications exercise and starts becoming management.

Meridian Perspective

Don't judge the strategy by the report alone.

A good report can tell us what an organisation says, measures and discloses.

To understand the strategy, look at what happens when there is pressure.

What gets funded? What gets stopped? Who gets held accountable? Which risks reach the board? Which decisions become different?

“The real test of a sustainability strategy is not what the organisation says when things are going well. It is what changes when the choices become difficult.”

Five questions worth asking

Perhaps the next time a sustainability report lands on the table, the conversation should move beyond whether the report is good.

Ask instead:

1

What has actually changed?

Look for changes in decisions, behaviour and investment — not just new initiatives.

2

Who owns the outcome?

If everything belongs to the sustainability team, perhaps it does not yet belong to the business.

3

What happens when commercial pressure arrives?

This is often where the real priorities become visible.

4

Are the hardest issues being discussed?

A strategy should not only celebrate progress. It should make difficult conversations possible.

5

Would the strategy still matter if nobody had to publish a report?

That question can reveal more than another page of disclosure.

A sustainability report can be polished, comprehensive and credible — and still leave the most important question unanswered.

What changed because of it?

That is where reporting ends and strategy begins.
