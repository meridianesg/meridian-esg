CSR, Sustainability & ESG: What's the Difference?

Three terms. Three different histories. One increasingly connected business agenda.

If you have ever wondered whether CSR, sustainability and ESG are simply different names for the same thing, you are not alone. They overlap. But they are not identical. Understanding the difference helps explain how corporate responsibility evolved — and why today's sustainability agenda is increasingly connected to strategy, risk, governance and value creation.

01 — Where CSR came from

Corporate Social Responsibility, or CSR, emerged from the idea that businesses have responsibilities beyond generating profit.

CSR traditionally focused on how a company behaves toward society — including employees, communities, philanthropy, ethical conduct and social impacts.

For many organisations, CSR became associated with community programmes, charitable contributions, employee volunteering and social investment.

CSR often asked: “What responsibility does business have toward society?”

02 — Sustainability enters business

Sustainability introduced a broader question.

Instead of looking only at social responsibility, organisations increasingly had to consider how economic, environmental and social factors affected their ability to operate and create value over the long term.

This brought issues such as climate change, natural resources, pollution, labour conditions, human rights and community impacts into a wider business conversation.

The emphasis began moving from individual CSR programmes toward the sustainability of the organisation itself and the systems in which it operates.

01

CSR

Responsibility toward society and stakeholders, often expressed through programmes, policies and social initiatives.

02

Sustainability

The ability to create long-term value while considering economic, environmental and social impacts.

03

ESG

A framework for understanding environmental, social and governance factors that can affect organisations and investment decisions.

03 — The rise of ESG

ESG — Environmental, Social and Governance — brought another important dimension into the conversation.

Rather than asking only whether a company is responsible, ESG increasingly asks how environmental, social and governance factors can affect organisational performance, risk, resilience and value.

This helped move sustainability closer to investors, financial markets, boards and mainstream corporate decision-making.

The three letters themselves are not the destination. They are a way of organising information and thinking about issues that may be financially and strategically relevant.

04 — What actually changed?

The biggest change was not the arrival of another acronym.

It was the gradual movement of sustainability from the margins of corporate activity toward the centre of business decision-making.

Earlier CSR thinking

“Are we doing something good for society?”

Community investment, philanthropy, employee programmes and social initiatives were often the visible face of corporate responsibility.

Sustainability thinking

“How can the organisation operate sustainably?”

Environmental and social impacts became increasingly connected to operations, resources, stakeholders and long-term resilience.

ESG thinking

“What environmental, social and governance factors matter to the organisation and its stakeholders?”

The conversation increasingly entered boardrooms, investment decisions, risk management and corporate reporting.

05 — Why companies still get confused

The problem is that the terms have evolved at different times, in different communities and for different purposes.

CSR, sustainability and ESG can therefore coexist inside the same organisation — sometimes without anyone being entirely clear about how they relate to each other.

A company may have a CSR department, a sustainability strategy, an ESG committee and an annual sustainability report.

That does not necessarily mean it has a coherent sustainability approach.

The presence of ESG structures does not automatically mean sustainability is embedded in the business.

06 — From reporting to strategy

This is where the distinction becomes important for management.

A company can produce a sophisticated sustainability report while its sustainability strategy remains weak.

It can have ambitious targets without a credible implementation plan.

It can establish governance structures without creating genuine accountability.

And it can communicate impressive commitments without changing the decisions that drive environmental and social outcomes.

The real test is therefore not the sophistication of the terminology.

The real test is whether sustainability influences how the organisation makes decisions.

The Meridian Perspective

Don't get trapped by the labels.

CSR, sustainability and ESG represent different stages in the evolution of corporate thinking. But organisations sometimes spend too much energy deciding which label to use — and too little asking whether the underlying issues are actually being managed.

A strong sustainability approach should connect responsibility, impact, risk, opportunity, governance and business strategy.

The better question is not “Are we doing ESG?”

It is: “Is sustainability changing the way we run the business?”

07 — Five questions for management

01

What sustainability issues are genuinely material to our business?

Not every issue deserves equal attention.

02

Who is accountable for delivering our sustainability commitments?

Responsibility without accountability rarely produces sustained results.

03

Are our targets supported by a credible implementation plan?

Ambition is not the same as execution.

04

What has actually changed in our business because of sustainability?

Look for changes in decisions, investment, operations and behaviour.

05

Would our stakeholders recognise the difference?

Ultimately, sustainability is experienced in the real world — not only in reports.
