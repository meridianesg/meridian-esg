When sustainability commitments meet commercial reality.

A target is easy to announce. The more interesting question is what happens when achieving it becomes difficult, expensive or inconvenient.

September 2026 Strategy · Markets · Transition Meridian ESG

There is no shortage of sustainability commitments.

Net-zero targets have become familiar. Companies publish climate ambitions, renewable-energy targets, responsible sourcing commitments and social goals.

The difficult part comes later.

What happens when the commitment meets a business decision?

The signal

Sustainability is increasingly being connected to investment, financing, risk and long-term competitiveness across Southeast Asia.

The commitment is only the beginning.

ASEAN's economic strategy for 2026–2030 explicitly links sustainable finance with investment, capital allocation and the region's competitiveness. It also identifies the need to mobilise financing for sustainable sectors and support an orderly transition. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

In Indonesia, OJK's Sustainable Capital Market Roadmap 2026–2030 similarly places sustainable financing and investment within the broader development of the capital market. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

That changes the context around corporate commitments.

Sustainability is increasingly being discussed alongside questions about investment, risk, financing and business resilience.

Then comes the uncomfortable part.

Suppose reducing emissions requires additional capital expenditure.

Suppose a supplier with a lower environmental footprint costs more.

Suppose changing a production process slows output in the short term.

Suppose the cleaner technology is not yet commercially competitive.

None of these questions make the sustainability commitment irrelevant.

They make it real.

The tension

A sustainability strategy becomes meaningful when it influences decisions that have something at stake.

This is where targets can be misleading.

A target tells us where an organisation says it wants to go.

It does not necessarily tell us how the organisation intends to get there.

Two companies can announce similar ambitions and face very different realities because their assets, markets, supply chains, customers, financing conditions and operating environments are different.

That is particularly important in Southeast Asia, where economies and industries are at very different stages of transition.

The real test is not the announcement.

The more revealing evidence often sits somewhere else.

Look at capital allocation.

Look at procurement.

Look at incentives.

Look at who has responsibility when a target is missed.

Look at what happens when the commercial case and the sustainability case point in different directions.

What is changing

Regional sustainable-finance frameworks are increasingly connecting sustainability with investment, capital allocation and economic transition.

The business question

Does the sustainability commitment influence decisions about capital, operations, procurement and risk?

What to watch

The distance between what companies promise publicly and what their internal decision-making actually rewards.

Meridian Interpretation

The strongest sustainability strategies are tested by trade-offs.

If nothing important has to change, the strategy has not really been tested.

This does not mean every sustainability decision should automatically override commercial considerations.

Businesses have to remain financially viable. Investments have to make sense. Employees, customers, suppliers and shareholders all have legitimate interests.

The more useful question is whether sustainability has become part of the decision itself.

When a major investment is being considered, is the sustainability dimension visible?

When suppliers are selected, does it matter?

When executives are evaluated, is progress relevant?

When a difficult trade-off appears, does someone have the authority to raise the issue?

That is where a sustainability commitment starts becoming a management system rather than a statement.
